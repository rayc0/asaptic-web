Why Factory Sourcing Often Requires a Deposit First

By Asaptic Labs2026-06-137 min read

In direct factory sourcing, requiring a deposit upfront does not automatically mean a supplier is untrustworthy. For many manufacturers, a deposit is how they start material procurement, lock in production capacity, schedule the production line, and cover custom engineering costs. The real risk isn't the deposit itself — it's a deposit that isn't tied to verifiable specifications, documentation, inspection rights, and payment milestones.

For informational purposes only — this is not legal, tax, or trade-finance advice. Cross-border payment, contract enforcement, refunds, and dispute handling depend on the transaction structure, applicable law, banking, logistics, and the supplier entity involved.

Why do factories require a deposit?

Once a factory accepts an order, it typically needs to procure raw materials, reserve line time, arrange tooling or jigs, assign engineers, and absorb inventory risk if the buyer cancels. In-stock standard products may qualify for a low deposit or cash-on-delivery, but custom parts, small-batch pilot runs, medical devices, electronic components, BESS parts, photonics components, or anything requiring a dedicated BOM will usually require a deposit.

Deposits also filter out non-serious buyers. For high-inquiry-volume factories, a "purchase intent" with no deposit attached is hard to distinguish from a real order. Buyers should accept this commercial reality while turning the deposit from an unconditional prepayment into a bounded project-kickoff payment.

What should be locked down before paying a deposit?

  • The exact product specification, drawings, BOM, model number, revision, and approved-substitution rules.
  • The sample-approval process — who approves it, the acceptance criteria, and how any gap between sample and mass production is handled.
  • Quality standards, AQL or functional test criteria, sampling method, and how non-conformances are handled.
  • The production schedule, key milestones, delivery date, and delay-notification obligations.
  • Third-party or buyer inspection rights, and the rework, re-inspection, and payment-hold rules that apply if inspection fails.
  • Shipping documentation: commercial invoice, packing list, certificate of origin, test reports, compliance certificates, and logistics data.
  • Balance-payment terms — for example, "payable after pre-shipment inspection passes" rather than an unconditional due date.

Deposit red flags

Requiring a deposit is not itself a red flag, but you should pause if: the supplier refuses to disclose its business entity, registered address, bank-account holder, or export qualifications; the receiving account doesn't match the contracting entity and the explanation is unconvincing; the quote is extremely low but demands a high deposit percentage; the supplier refuses samples or inspection; the contract omits specs, delivery date, and non-conformance handling; payment is requested to a personal account or an untraceable channel; or you're pressured to pay same-day with no documentation provided.

For medical, electrical, energy-storage, photonics, or other regulated/high-tech products, also verify concrete evidence such as ISO 13485, IEC 60601-1, UL/IEC test results, the ARTG/MDL/CE/FDA pathway, ECCN/EAR99, or destination-country certification. Registration numbers, licenses, and certification status should be checked against live registries — not just a PDF screenshot.

How to turn a deposit into a manageable risk

Buyers should treat the deposit as the ticket into a controlled execution process — not as purchasing trust. Verify the supplier's entity and bank account first, then bundle the purchase order, spec attachment, quality agreement, inspection plan, and payment terms into a single deal package. After the deposit is paid, require the supplier to provide material-procurement proof, production photos, key test records, and packaging confirmation at each milestone.

A genuinely capable supplier will usually accept clear milestones and inspection mechanisms, because those mechanisms also reduce rework and disputes on their side. If a supplier will only accept "pay first, sort out the rest later," buyers should treat that as a procurement-process failure — not a difference in negotiating style.

Frequently asked questions

Does a factory requiring a deposit always signal risk?

Not necessarily. Deposits are commonly used to lock in capacity, purchase materials, and start custom production. The risk lies in a deposit that isn't tied to a verifiable specification, documentation, inspection, and milestones.

What deposit percentage is reasonable?

There's no universal percentage. 30% deposit / 70% before shipment is common, but custom development, tooling, or scarce materials may differ. The percentage should match the supplier's verification level, the resalability of materials, and the strength of inspection controls.

How can buyers reduce deposit risk?

Verify the supplier's entity and bank account before paying, and tie the deposit to sample approval, the BOM, quality standards, the production schedule, inspection rights, shipping documentation, and balance-payment terms.

Should buyers refuse deposits outright?

For in-stock standard products, buyers can push for a low deposit or payment after delivery. For custom production, refusing deposits outright may screen out genuine manufacturers. A more practical strategy is to control deposit terms and milestones.
