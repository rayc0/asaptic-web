Robot sourcing guide

AJM joint module vs. off-the-shelf servo: a sizing comparison

An AJM-class integrated joint module combines the harmonic reducer, frameless torque motor, encoder, and driver into one assembled joint unit. A separate servo-plus-gearbox build can be attractive when your team already knows the parts, wants maximum freedom over each component, or is building only a small prototype. The trade-off is that you now own the mechanical packaging, backlash control, cable routing, tuning, thermal checks, supplier coordination, and first-pass validation. Integrated modules usually win when torque density, compact packaging, clean documentation, and single-vendor accountability matter more than choosing every subcomponent yourself.

What "integrated joint module" actually means

In a robot arm, a joint is not just a motor. It is a torque source, transmission, position sensing system, power stage, signal interface, mechanical housing, and thermal path. An integrated joint module packages those pieces into one unit so the robot builder can specify a joint by output torque, frame size, interface, and documentation instead of managing a small subsystem every time an axis is added.

An AJM-class module is built around the same functional stack used in many precision robot joints: a compact harmonic reducer, a frameless torque motor, an encoder set, and an integrated servo driver. The important point is not that those parts are impossible to buy separately. The point is that their fit, alignment, wiring, tuning, and test data are delivered as one sourcing item.

That has practical consequences. A procurement line item can map to an engineering function. A replacement part can map to a known frame and torque class. A support question can be routed to one accountable supplier instead of being split between a motor vendor, gearbox vendor, encoder vendor, driver vendor, and the internal engineer who assembled the joint.

Where off-the-shelf servo+gearbox wins

A separate servo and gearbox can still be the right answer. For very low-volume prototypes, a team may already have shelves of familiar motors, drives, and reducers. The control stack may already know those drives. The mechanical team may prefer to design its own housings and bearing supports. In that context, the cost of learning a new integrated module may be higher than the benefit.

The separate approach also gives maximum component-level freedom. You can choose a motor winding, encoder style, reduction ratio, housing geometry, connector family, or control interface independently. For research rigs, test stands, student projects, and non-final prototypes, that freedom can be useful because the goal is learning and iteration rather than repeatable sourcing.

The hidden cost is integration. The gearbox must be mounted without misalignment. The motor has to be coupled correctly. The encoder reference must be stable. The driver must be tuned. Backlash, stiffness, heat, cable strain, ingress protection, and service access all become design tasks. If the joint underperforms, the team has to determine whether the issue is mechanical fit, control tuning, component selection, or a supplier quality problem.

Where an integrated module wins

An integrated module wins when the joint is a means to an end. If the application is inspection, pick-and-place, tool handling, mobile manipulation, or production testing, the team often wants a compact axis that reaches the required torque and stiffness without becoming its own mechanical engineering project.

Packaging is the first advantage. A module designed as a joint can place the reducer, motor, encoder, driver, and connectors around the output axis in a compact envelope. That can improve torque density at the robot level, not just at the motor level, because there is less bracketry, coupling, adapter hardware, and wiring volume around every axis.

Backlash and stiffness are the next advantages. A precision reducer still has to be selected and loaded correctly, but a validated module should arrive with the mechanical stack already matched. That makes it easier to compare output behavior between joints and easier to ask whether the module is suitable for a shoulder, elbow, or wrist role.

The simplest advantage is administrative: one part number. With one module, the quote, drawing, test data, firmware notes, warranty terms, and replacement plan all point to the same item. That matters when a prototype becomes a repeat build. Engineering can spend less time recreating a joint package, and operations can keep a clearer spares list.

Sizing math is identical either way

The physics does not care whether you buy a finished joint module or combine a servo and gearbox yourself. The output shaft still needs enough torque for the payload, reach, tool mass, structure mass, and dynamic margin. A separate motor and gearbox can be undersized; an integrated module can also be undersized. The right comparison starts at the joint output.

For the actuator-selection framework and the full first-pass torque derivation, read Robot 101: actuators and joint modules. Use that sizing step to compare the two sourcing paths on equal terms: required output torque, allowable backlash, stiffness, duty cycle, thermal behavior, documentation, and support.

After that, the decision becomes operational. A separate servo-plus-gearbox approach may be cheaper on a narrow component list but more expensive after engineering time and validation. An integrated module may look higher per part but lower risk when repeatability, spares, and support are included. Quick answers
What is the difference between an integrated actuator module and a servo plus gearbox?
An integrated module packages the reducer, motor, encoder, and driver as one validated joint unit. A servo-plus-gearbox build sources those functions separately and leaves integration to the robot builder.
When does a servo plus gearbox make sense for a robot joint?
It can make sense for very low-volume prototypes, lab fixtures, and teams already invested in familiar parts and controls. The trade-off is more packaging, tuning, and validation work.
Why choose an integrated joint module for a robot arm?
Integrated modules simplify torque-class selection, mechanical packaging, supplier coordination, support, and spares planning under one part number.
